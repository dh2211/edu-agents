# Grade 11 English: argument writing

> **Teacher request.** Grade 11 English, 55-minute period. Teach students to explain how evidence supports a claim in an argument paragraph. Standard: CCSS W.11-12.1. Then a rubric for the paragraph, feedback on one sample paragraph, supports for four English learners at intermediate proficiency and two students with extended time in their plans, and a short email to families about the argument unit.

## Lesson plan (`edu-lesson-planner`)

**Objective (student language).** I can write an argument paragraph where I explain how each piece of evidence proves my claim.

**Success criteria**

- My claim is one arguable sentence.
- I use at least two pieces of evidence and cite where each comes from.
- After each piece of evidence, I explain how it supports my claim.

**Warm-up (7 min).** Project: "Claim: Our school should start at 8:30 a.m. Evidence: Many students are tired." Ask: "Is this convincing? What's missing?" Students write one sentence, then share with a partner. Collect two answers. Steer toward "it doesn't say why being tired matters" and "where does that come from?"

**I do (12 min).** Introduce claim, evidence, reasoning. Model with a paragraph on the same topic using one real source the teacher provides [verify: teacher to supply the source and quotation]. Think aloud at each sentence:

- "My claim is arguable: someone could disagree."
- "Here is my evidence. I name the source so readers can trust it."
- "Now the step most writers skip. I explain how this evidence proves my claim. I ask myself: so what? Why does this fact mean the school should change?"

Highlight claim in one color, evidence in another, reasoning in a third.

**We do (12 min).** Show a paragraph with a claim and two pieces of evidence but no reasoning. The class writes the reasoning sentences together. Ask: "What would someone who disagrees say? Does our reasoning answer them?" Likely weak answer: restating the evidence ("This shows students are tired"). Push: "Why does that matter for the claim?"

**You do (18 min).** Students choose one of three provided claims and a short source packet [teacher supplies packet], then write one paragraph with two pieces of evidence and reasoning after each.

**Check for understanding.**

- During "we do": students write one reasoning sentence on a sticky note. The teacher sorts them into "explains" and "restates" piles at a glance and reads one of each aloud.
- Exit ticket: students highlight their own reasoning sentences and write "My weakest link is ___ because ___."

**Closure (6 min).** Partners swap paragraphs, find the reasoning, and check it against the success criteria.

**Differentiation.** See the `edu-differentiation` section below.

**Materials.** Model paragraph, we-do paragraph, three claims, source packet, highlighters, sticky notes, rubric student checklist.

Minutes: 7 + 12 + 12 + 18 + 6 = 55.

## Rubric (`edu-rubric-builder`)

| Criterion | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Claim | States one precise, arguable claim and names its scope. | States one arguable claim. | States a claim that is a fact or too broad to argue. | No clear claim. |
| Evidence | Uses two or more relevant pieces of evidence from the sources, each cited and integrated into the sentence. | Uses two relevant, cited pieces of evidence. | Uses one piece of evidence, or evidence without a citation. | Uses no evidence, or evidence unrelated to the claim. |
| Reasoning | After each piece of evidence, explains how it proves the claim and answers a likely objection. | After each piece of evidence, explains how it proves the claim. | Restates or summarizes the evidence instead of explaining it. | No reasoning. |
| Conventions | Errors are rare and never get in the way of meaning. | A few errors; meaning stays clear. | Errors sometimes make meaning unclear. | Errors often make meaning unclear. |

**Student checklist**

- My claim is one sentence someone could disagree with.
- I used two pieces of evidence and said where each came from.
- After each piece of evidence, I explained how it proves my claim.
- I reread for errors that would confuse a reader.

Total: 16 points.

## Feedback on a sample paragraph (`edu-student-work-feedback`)

**Invented student paragraph (Student A)**

> High schools should start at 8:30 or later. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommended in 2014 that middle and high schools start no earlier than 8:30 a.m. Also, in our class survey, 19 of 27 students said they feel tired in first period. This shows that students are tired and the school should change.

**Feedback**

- **What works.** Your claim is clear and arguable, and your first piece of evidence comes from a source readers will trust, a national doctors' group. You also gathered your own data from the class survey, which makes the argument local.
- **Next step.** Your last sentence restates the evidence ("students are tired"). After each piece of evidence, add a sentence that explains why it proves the school should start later. For example, after the AAP evidence, explain why a doctors' recommendation should matter to a school board.
- **Question.** Someone could say students would just stay up later if school started later. How would you answer them?

**Suggested score (for the teacher to confirm)**

| Criterion | Level | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Claim | 3 | Arguable, but scope ("high schools") is broad. |
| Evidence | 4 | Two relevant pieces, both identified, integrated into sentences. |
| Reasoning | 2 | The final sentence restates the evidence. |
| Conventions | 4 | No errors that affect meaning. |

Suggested total: 13 of 16. [verify: the AAP 2014 recommendation is accurate as the student states it, but check the source in your packet.]

## Differentiation (`edu-differentiation`)

**UDL changes to the lesson**

- Engagement: three claim choices let students pick a topic they care about.
- Representation: the model paragraph is color-coded, and the teacher reads it aloud.
- Action and expression: students may talk their reasoning through with a partner before they write.

**Support version (on-level objective, more scaffolding)**

- A claim-evidence-reasoning organizer with three boxes per piece of evidence.
- Reasoning frames: "This shows that ___, which matters because ___." "If ___ is true, then ___."
- A shorter source packet with key sentences highlighted.

**English learners at intermediate proficiency**

- Pre-teach four words with examples: claim, evidence, reasoning, arguable.
- Give the reasoning frames above and a frame for citing: "According to ___, ___."
- Partner talk before writing, with a partner who shares a first language if the teacher confirms that helps.
- Same objective and same rubric. Score conventions on whether errors block meaning.

**Extension**

- Add a counterclaim and a rebuttal sentence, scored as the "answers a likely objection" part of the level 4 reasoning row.

**Accommodations menu** (apply only where a student's plan calls for them)

| Type | Options |
| --- | --- |
| Timing | Extended time on the "you do" paragraph; finish during the next class's warm-up |
| Presentation | Source packet read aloud or in audio; larger print |
| Response | Speech-to-text or typed paragraph |
| Setting | Small-group table during "you do" |

These are all accommodations. None changes the objective, so none is a modification.
